Medical condensor · metric selection

Eleven transcripts, one careful human edit each, and a systematic test of which scoring metric actually rewards good condensing

Every metric in this project's toolkit was tested against the same yardstick: a real, clinically-complete human condensation of each transcript, compared against random word deletion at the exact same length. A metric worth trusting should score the careful edit close to unchanged and the random deletion clearly worse. Not all of them do.

Date 2026‑07‑31 Files 11 real transcripts, manually condensed, saved to optimised transcript for metric testing/ Metrics tested 8, across 3 families (KDE, cosine, ROUGE-1)

The test design

Eleven real transcripts (prim1, 10–19) were condensed by hand — every symptom, history detail, medication, allergy, and plan item kept, only genuine filler removed (greetings, repeated acknowledgments, false starts). Each was saved to a real project folder, optimised transcript for metric testing/, at 41–60% of the original word count.

For each file, three variants were scored against the real SOAP note using every metric available: the original transcript, the optimised (careful, content-aware) condensation, and a random word-deletion control sampled to the exact same word count as the optimised version. A metric that's actually measuring condensation quality should show the optimised version losing little to nothing relative to the original, while the random version — same length, no judgment applied — loses meaningfully more. The gap between those two diffs, averaged across all 11 files, is that metric's discriminative power: how well it tells good editing apart from careless deletion at a fixed length.

The catch in the raw averages

KDE omission's average is real, but it isn't robust

An average alone doesn't show whether a metric is reliably discriminating or whether one large number is doing all the work. Checking every one of the 11 files individually: ROUGE-1 recall, cosine recall, and cosine F1 all got the direction right (optimised loses less than random) in 11 out of 11 files, with no exceptions. KDE omission got the direction right in most files, but produced several exact 0.0000 ties (both variants scoring identically, i.e. no signal at all) and one wildly disproportionate value: on prim12.txt, the random-deletion control scored a diff of −0.8954 — roughly 10× larger in magnitude than every other value this metric produced across all 11 files, all 33 scored texts.

Verified, not dismissed as a glitch Checked directly: this wasn't a computation error or a degenerate input (432 words embedded from the random sample, 78 from the SOAP note — both reasonably sized). It's a genuine, if statistically unusual, score for that specific random word combination and whatever bandwidth the KDE's cross-validated grid search happened to select for it. Real, but exactly the kind of high-variance behavior that makes a metric's average untrustworthy: excluding just this one file, KDE omission's discriminative gap drops from 0.103 to roughly 0.023 — weaker than cosine F1, nowhere close to ROUGE-1 recall. One file's random sample was doing most of the work in the original average.

This has a clean structural explanation, worth recording since it clarifies why omission_transcript_to_soap and groundedness_soap_in_transcript (both already documented elsewhere in this project as coming from the same check_omissions function) behave so differently under length changes. Both scores are a ratio: a density value divided by a normalizing floor. For groundedness_soap_in_transcript, that floor is the CANDIDATE transcript's own weakest self-density — which is exactly what shrinks and destabilizes as the candidate is condensed more aggressively, and is the root cause of the severe length bias documented in this project's other report. For omission_transcript_to_soap, the floor is the SOAP note's own weakest self-density instead — and the SOAP note never changes across the three variants being compared, so that floor stays fixed. It's a real structural improvement over groundedness, just not a large enough one: the metric's numerator still depends on the candidate's own point cloud, which is enough to introduce the kind of high-variance instability seen on prim12.txt.

Verdict

Ranked: which metric to actually trust

  1. ROUGE-1 recall — the strongest, most consistent discriminator tested: largest gap (0.111), correct in all 11 files, and it's the cheapest metric here by far — no embedding model, no density estimate, just word-overlap counting. The one caveat: it can't credit a paraphrase or synonym, so it will slightly undercount genuinely good condensation that happens to reword content.
  2. Cosine F1 / cosine recall — the best semantic-aware pair: also correct in all 11 files, with cosine F1's average diff for the optimised condensation landing at essentially +0.001 — as close to "lost nothing" as a real, non-degenerate condensation can score. Recommended alongside ROUGE-1 recall specifically because it's methodologically independent (embeddings vs exact words) — agreement between the two is real triangulation, not two views of the same computation.
  3. KDE omission — usable as a secondary signal, not a primary one. Its average looks reasonable but a single file's high-variance random-control score was responsible for most of that average; the metric produced multiple exact-zero ties elsewhere, suggesting weak sensitivity in the typical case.
  4. Precision-only metrics (cosine precision, ROUGE-1 precision) and ROUGE-1 F1 — correct direction, but a small, unreliable gap on their own. Removing almost any words tends to raise apparent "precision" a little, regardless of whether the removal was smart, so precision alone barely separates good editing from careless deletion. Still useful as one half of an F1 pairing, just not standalone.
  5. KDE groundedness — confirmed, again, as the metric to avoid for this purpose. Both the optimised and random variants score deeply negative (−20.6 and −21.6 respectively) on a metric whose typical range is nowhere near that scale for an original transcript — the gap between them (0.92) is under 5% of the scale it's measured on. This is consistent with, and adds a second independent confirmation of, the length-bias finding already documented for this metric in this project's other report.
✓ Recommendation for evaluating this project's NLP condensers going forward: lead with ROUGE-1 recall and cosine F1 together, treat KDE omission as a secondary cross-check at most, and don't use KDE groundedness to judge condensation quality — only to track it for historical continuity with earlier results.
